Before the arrival of Europeans, women in Mesoamerican societies held complex roles that balanced domestic duties with vital economic and spiritual responsibilities. In Aztec and Maya cultures, society viewed the universe through a lens of gender complementarity, meaning men and women had distinct but equally valued functions necessary to maintain cosmic balance. While men primarily engaged in warfare and external politics, women managed households, spearheaded textile production, and dominated local marketplaces. Textiles, in particular, served as a primary form of tribute and currency, placing women at the center of the economic system. Furthermore, women frequently served as priestesses, healers, and midwives, acting as crucial intermediaries between the community and the divine.

The Spanish conquest and subsequent colonial era drastically altered these dynamics by imposing a patriarchal European structure. The introduction of Roman Catholic traditions and Spanish law restricted women's legal autonomy, emphasizing the domestic ideals of marianismo—which championed female submissiveness, purity, and self-sacrifice. Despite these rigid constraints, Mexican women consistently pushed against the boundaries of colonial society. Figures like Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz, a 17th-century nun, became one of the greatest literary minds of the Americas by fiercely defending a woman's right to education and intellectual pursuit. During the early 19th century, women also played pivotal roles in the Mexican War of Independence; leaders like Josefa Ortiz de Domínguez risked their lives and fortunes to organize underground resistance movements and smuggle critical intelligence to insurgent forces.

The 20th century marked a dramatic shift toward political mobilization and formal equality, accelerated heavily by the Mexican Revolution of 1910. Thousands of women, known as soldaderas, traveled with the armies to serve as nurses, cooks, smugglers, and even frontline combatants. This massive social upheaval laid the groundwork for the modern feminist movement in Mexico. Decades of organized activism culminated in women securing the right to vote in national elections in 1953. In recent decades, Mexican women have continued to break political barriers, leading to the implementation of strict gender parity laws in Congress and, ultimately, the historic election of Claudia Sheinbaum as Mexico's first female president in 2024, signaling a monumental evolution in the nation's leadership.
